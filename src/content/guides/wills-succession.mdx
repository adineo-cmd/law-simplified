## The short version

A will is the cheapest legal document with the largest consequence you will ever sign. It needs no stamp paper, no notary, and no registration. What it needs is clarity: who gets what, signed by you in the presence of two witnesses who also sign. Everything else — lawyers, registration, probate — is machinery around that core, useful in proportion to how contested your estate might be.

## What makes a will valid

- **In writing**, any language, on any paper — typed and printed is normal and safer than handwritten ambiguity.
- **Signed by you**, and attested by **two witnesses** who saw you sign (they should not be beneficiaries, or their share is contested).
- **Made by an adult of sound mind**, without coercion — a doctor's note alongside helps where age or illness will be questioned.
- **A recent date**, and ideally a line revoking earlier wills.

The classic failure is not formality — it is ambiguity. "House to my son" when there are two sons, joint accounts left unaddressed, nominees mentioned as if they were heirs. Name assets precisely, name people fully, and state what happens if a beneficiary predeceases you.

## The nominee trap

Nominations (bank accounts, shares, insurance, society records) make collection easy — and settle nothing permanently. A nominee holds the asset for the **legal heirs**; they are a postman, not an owner, except where the law says otherwise (insurance is paid to the nominee outright; shares and bank deposits go through the nominee to the succession). A will aligns the two; without one, the nominee and the heirs can be different people with different claims.

## Registration and probate, honestly

- **Registration** is optional. A registered will is harder to allege as forged and lives in the sub-registrar's custody — worth it for estates likely to be contested.
- **Probate** — court certification of the will — is mandatory only in the Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai metropolitan areas (and for wills there of property), and customary for some communities. Elsewhere it is optional; a will with clean attestation usually works through banks and registrars with death certificate plus indemnities.
- **A safe custody plan** matters more than either: a will nobody can find is a will without one. Tell the executor where it is.

## Dying without one: the succession formula

Intestate succession is a statutory formula, not a family discussion. Under the Hindu Succession Act, Class I heirs (mother, widow, sons, daughters, and specified descendants) take per capita and per stirpes in defined shares; other religions have their own schemes under their statutes. Two consequences surprise every family: a daughter's equal share (settled law, including in agricultural and coparcenary property), and the in-laws' position — a widow shares with her mother-in-law, not instead of her.

For a spouse and children, the practical effect of no will is rarely "the family gets nothing" — it is "the family gets a formula, plus paperwork, plus the litigation risk of anyone disagreeing."

## The three papers the family needs afterwards

1. **Death certificate** — the key that opens everything.
2. **Legal heir certificate / succession certificate** — issued by the tehsildar (heir certificate) or civil court (succession certificate for debts and securities); banks and employers ask for these to release assets without a will.
3. **Probate or letters of administration** where the estate is large, the will needs certification, or an institution insists.

Apply early: heirship paperwork is the slowest part of every estate, and grief is the worst time to discover which certificates your banks require.

## The trap to avoid

Do not make one will and never revisit it. Marriages, children, property, and friendships change; a will that predates the grandchildren or the flat you bought in 2019 creates the litigation it was meant to prevent. A five-minute annual review — "is this still true?" — is the maintenance the document needs.

## Your next move

1. List your assets and accounts in one place — the inventory is the will's raw material.
2. Draft it plainly: full names, precise assets, clear shares, a residuary clause, two non-beneficiary witnesses.
3. Decide on registration based on contest risk; store it where the executor can find it.
4. Align nominations with the will — change them where the two disagree.
5. Tell the executor and family the will's location, and review it every year.
