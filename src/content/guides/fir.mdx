## The short version

An FIR — First Information Report — is the document the police register when they receive information about a cognizable offence. A cognizable offence is one serious enough that the police can investigate without a magistrate’s permission.

The law does not require you to be a lawyer, to know the section number, or to have a perfect story. It requires you to report the facts. The police are supposed to register the FIR, read it back to you, and give you a free copy.

## What to bring

Bring whatever you have:

- your identity proof;
- a written complaint with dates, times, places, and names;
- photographs, messages, medical records, receipts, or CCTV locations;
- contact details of witnesses;
- a pen and a notebook, or your phone, to note the FIR number and the officer’s name.

If you cannot write, the police should record your oral complaint. You can also ask someone to help you write it.

## At the police station

1. **Ask for the officer on duty** and state that you want to file an FIR for a cognizable offence.
2. **Give the facts chronologically.** Start with the date, time, and place. Then say what happened, who did it, and what you saw or heard.
3. **Do not guess.** If you do not know a detail, say so. The investigation can fill gaps; a false detail can damage your credibility.
4. **Read the FIR before signing.** Ask for corrections to be made and initialled. Never sign a blank page.
5. **Ask for a free copy.** You are entitled to it.
6. **Note the FIR number, police station, and officer’s name.** Keep the copy safe.

<Callout tone="tip" title="Zero FIR">
If the incident happened outside the police station’s jurisdiction, the station can still register a Zero FIR and transfer it to the correct station. The point is to make sure the complaint is recorded, not to send you from station to station.
</Callout>

## If the police refuse

Refusal is not the end of the process. It is a step that needs a record.

1. **Ask for the refusal in writing.** The officer may not give it. Note the officer’s name, badge number, date, time, and what was said.
2. **Send a written complaint to the Superintendent of Police** (or the senior officer for that area) by email, registered post, or in person. Attach your complaint and any evidence.
3. **Approach the Magistrate under the BNSS, 2023.** If the police still do not register the FIR, you can file a complaint before the Magistrate, who can direct an investigation.
4. **Contact a legal aid lawyer** if you cannot afford one. Your District Legal Services Authority can help with the complaint and the court process.

Keep copies of everything you send. A refusal to register an FIR is itself a serious allegation; the paper trail is what makes it reviewable.

## If you are the accused

This guide is written for complainants, but the same encounter can look very different from the other side. If you are detained or arrested:

- ask for the grounds of arrest;
- ask to inform a family member or friend;
- ask for a lawyer;
- ask for a medical examination if you have injuries;
- do not resist or sign blank papers;
- remember that you must be produced before a Magistrate within 24 hours.

The D.K. Basu guidelines laid down by the Supreme Court require an arrest memo, information to family, a medical examination, and access to a lawyer. They are not a substitute for legal advice, but they are a useful checklist.

## What not to do

- Do not exaggerate or invent details to make the offence sound more serious.
- Do not sign an FIR you have not read.
- Do not hand over your only copy of the evidence.
- Do not assume the police will contact you; follow up with the FIR number.
- Do not pay a “fixer” or “agent” to get an FIR registered.

## Your next move

1. Write the complaint in date order.
2. Go to the nearest police station and ask for an FIR.
3. Read it, correct it, sign it, and take the free copy.
4. If refused, record the refusal and escalate in writing.
5. Ask the Magistrate or a legal aid lawyer if the refusal continues.
