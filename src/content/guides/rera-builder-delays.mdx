## The short version

Before RERA, a delayed flat was a negotiation with an entity that held your money and your patience. After RERA (fully in force since May 2017), the promised possession date is a **statutory obligation**: miss it, and the buyer chooses between staying in with monthly interest and exiting with a full refund plus interest. The Act applies to residential and certain commercial projects above the size threshold, and its core promise is the one buyers care about — your money has a legal relationship with a date.

## What RERA changed for the buyer

- **The date is binding.** The possession date declared in the RERA registration (and your agreement) is enforceable. Extensions given by the authority (COVID-era, force majeure) are public on the portal — check which date is actually yours before complaining.
- **70% of your money is escrowed** to the project's construction account, proportionate to completion — the rule that made outright diversion of buyer funds harder.
- **Carpet area is the sales unit**, and the agreement-for-sale must follow the model format with the payment schedule linked to construction milestones.
- **Advance beyond 10%** of the price cannot be taken without a written and registered agreement.
- **Defect liability of five years** after possession: the builder must repair structural and workmanship defects reported within that window, at no cost, within 30 days of complaint.

## The delay remedy, precisely

For every month of delay past the due date, the builder owes you **interest at the prescribed rate** (state-specific — typically around the State Bank MCLR plus 2%) on everything you have paid. The same rate applies in both directions — the interest a builder charges you for instalment delay is the interest you charge them for possession delay, which was the Act's deliberate symmetry.

Your Section 18 choice on delay:

1. **Stay and claim interest** for the delay period, until possession — with all other agreement terms intact.
2. **Exit and claim refund** of the entire payment plus that interest, within the statutory timeline for repayment.

Calculate both before choosing: a project 18 months late with construction visibly moving may be worth the interest; a project stalled at slab level is worth the exit.

## Filing the complaint

Each state's RERA authority takes complaints **online** on its portal:

1. Register and file the complaint form — project registration number, agreement date, payments made with proof, the promised date, the delay.
2. Attach the agreement-for-sale, payment receipts, loan statements, and the builder's replies or the absence of them.
3. Relief sought: state the interest computation and the choice — possession with interest, or refund with interest.
4. Hearings are largely documentary; the authority can also fine the builder and register offences for continuing breaches. Appeals go to the Real Estate Appellate Tribunal, and then the High Court.

A lawyer is optional for a clean delay case — the documents speak. Where the builder contests the date, the agreement's clauses, or raises force majeure, take counsel.

## The force majeure debate

Builders raised COVID and "authority delays" as universal excuses. The settled position: extensions need to be **declared by the authority**, and the builder must show the specific cause affected the specific project. A blanket "the pandemic happened" does not freeze your interest clock — check your state RERA's actual extension orders for your project's district and period.

## If the builder is also in other trouble

Check the RERA portal's litigation column and search the project name with "insolvency" — if a homebuyer or creditor has taken the builder to **NCLT**, filing your RERA or NCLT claim within the insolvency window matters enormously: homebuyers are financial creditors with a defined place in the queue, and missing the claim deadline is the difference between recovery and a listed debt. Speed changes outcomes here more than anywhere else in this guide.

## The trap to avoid

Do not stop paying your home loan during the delay as leverage. The EMI is your obligation to the bank regardless; defaulting hands you a second problem (and a credit-report one) that has no bearing on the builder's liability. Claim the delay interest instead — that is the statutory lever.

## Your next move

1. Pull your project's RERA registration page — confirm the binding date and read the litigation column.
2. Build the file: agreement, payment proofs, loan statement, and every dated chasing email.
3. Compute the delay interest at your state's prescribed rate — both stay and exit scenarios.
4. File the complaint on the state RERA portal with the computation attached.
5. If insolvency looms, talk to a lawyer about the NCLT claim window the same week.
