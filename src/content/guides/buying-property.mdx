## The short version

Property disputes almost never begin on the day of the quarrel — they begin on the day of the purchase, when someone skipped a check to save a week. The checks are mechanical, most cost less than a lawyer's single opinion, and together they answer the only question that matters: does the seller actually own this, cleanly, and can you prove it later?

## The title search: thirty years, not one deed

Ask a lawyer (or a title-search service) to pull the property's record at the sub-registrar for **30 years**: every sale deed, gift, partition, release, mortgage, and will probated along the chain. What you are looking for:

- **Unbroken chain of title** — every transfer accounted for; a missing generation (an heir who never signed a release) is the classic defect that surfaces years later.
- **No pending litigation** — a **lis pendens** registered against the property means a case over it is running; buying under one inherits the case.
- **Joint ownership fully resolved** — every co-owner and legal heir of every deceased owner in the chain must have transferred their share. "My brother will sign later" is a lawsuit with a delay.

For agricultural land and older properties, also check revenue records (khata/10-1, jamabandi, patta) and land-ceiling restrictions, which vary by state.

## The encumbrance certificate

The **EC** from the sub-registrar lists every registered charge on the property — mortgages, liens, attachments — for the period you request (get 15–30 years). A bank loan on the property that the seller "forgot" appears here. If the seller's own home loan is being closed at sale, get the **discharge and the original deed returned** from that bank as a condition of payment, not a promise afterwards.

## For new projects: the RERA check

Every new and ongoing residential project must be **RERA-registered**, and the registration is searchable on your state's RERA portal — project name, promoter, approvals declared, litigation history, and the promised completion date. What to check before booking:

- **The registration exists** and covers the exact tower/phase you are buying.
- **The declared approvals** match what the sales team claims (sanctioned plan, land title, environmental clearances).
- **The litigation column** — RERA filings against the project are public.
- **The quoted carpet area** matches the RERA-filed plan — RERA made carpet area the sales unit precisely to end super-built-area pricing.

A builder selling an unregistered project, or collecting 10%+ of the price without a written agreement (RERA's rule), is already in breach — that is leverage you keep.

## The plan and the two certificates

- **Approved building plan** from the municipal authority — then verify the construction matches it (deviations can invite demolition or penalties).
- **Occupancy Certificate (OC)** — the authority's statement that the building is built per plan and safe to occupy. Buying without an OC means buying into water/power connection and regularisation fights.
- **Completion Certificate** where issued, and for plots, the layout approval.
- **Khata / mutation / property tax records in the seller's name** — tax receipts up to date is a quiet but decisive check; arrears become your problem at transfer.

## Money: the paperwork of paying

- **Stamp duty and registration** — typically 5–8% combined, state-specific; the sale deed **must be registered** (four months is the outer limit) to be enforceable against third parties.
- **TDS at 1%** on consideration above ₹50 lakh — deduct and deposit via Form 26QB. Skipping it makes the *buyer* liable for the seller's tax shortfall.
- **Payment trail**: everything through banking channels, matched to the deed. Cash components are the fastest way to lose both money and proof.
- **Home-loan diligence**: the bank's legal and technical verification is a second net — use their report, but do not treat it as a substitute for your own title search; the bank protects its loan, not your possession.

## Possession and after

At registration: collect the **original deed**, the bank's discharge (if any), the latest tax receipts, and the keys against a written handover note. Then **mutate** the property — khata transfer / municipal mutation — into your name; mutation is what makes the tax records and utilities follow you, and it is the step everyone defers until it inconveniences them.

## The trap to avoid

Do not buy on a **power of attorney** sale without extreme care — POA sales are the centre of gravity for property fraud, and several states have restricted them. If the "owner" cannot appear before the sub-registrar, find out why, and verify the POA itself at the registrar's record before paying anything.

## Your next move

1. Order the 30-year title search and the EC before the token, not after.
2. For new projects, pull the RERA registration and read its litigation tab.
3. Verify plan approval, OC/CC, and tax receipts in the seller's name.
4. Price the paperwork: stamp duty, registration, TDS, legal fees — 6–9% of the deal.
5. Register the deed, collect originals, and mutate the records the same month.

<Callout tone="note" title="This is general information">
Property law is intensely state-specific — duty rates, mutation systems, RERA rules, and land records differ everywhere. This checklist is the durable core; a local property lawyer should run the title opinion for anything at this price.
</Callout>
