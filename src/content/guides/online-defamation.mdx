## The short version

Someone posts something false about you — an accusation, a manipulated screenshot, a review that describes a crime you did not commit. Indian law gives you three instruments, and using them in the wrong order is how most people lose weeks: the platform's own machinery, a lawyer's notice, and the court's interim powers.

The legal test has not changed with the new codes: an imputation must be **false**, it must be **published** (posted, forwarded, shared), and it must **harm your reputation** in the eyes of right-thinking people. Truth for the public good, fair comment on public conduct, and court records are the standing defences — which is why "review of a business" and "false accusation of crime" are treated very differently.

## Hour one: preserve, do not engage

1. **Screenshot with context** — the URL, the post, the date and time visible, the account name and handle. A screenshot without a URL is an argument; with one, it is evidence.
2. **Save the share counts.** Reach matters later — for both compensation and the urgency of interim relief.
3. **Do not reply from the main account.** An angry reply does not undo the imputation, and everything you write becomes another exhibit.
4. **Do not threaten in the comments.** "My lawyer will destroy you" reads badly in front of a magistrate; a quiet, complete record reads well.

## Lane one: the platform's grievance officer

The IT Rules, 2021 require significant social media intermediaries to publish a **grievance officer** with response timelines — acknowledgement within 24 hours for content complaints, and resolution within set windows. File a report that says precisely: the URL, the specific false statement, the law it violates (defamation, impersonation, obscene or harassing content), and the remedy sought (removal or disabling access).

Platforms move fastest on impersonation, manipulated media, and clear policy violations. Defamation-adjacent posts sometimes survive policy review — that is what the notice and court lanes are for. Keep the ticket numbers: a platform that sat on a compliant report becomes part of your case.

## Lane two: the legal notice

A notice from an advocate — citing the post, the falsity, the harm, and the law — is the cheapest escalation that changes behaviour. It demands: deletion, a retraction or correction with equal visibility, and a written undertaking against repetition, within a stated deadline. Many posts die here, because the poster's own exposure (criminal complaint, damages suit) becomes real to them.

For a business attacked by fake reviews, the notice goes to both the poster and, where the platform knowingly hosts after a compliant complaint, the platform.

## Lane three: the court

Two moves matter, often combined:

- **An interim injunction / takedown order** — for posts that are actively spreading, the court can order removal and restraint from republication while the case runs. Reach evidence and the platform's non-response are what unlock this.
- **The substantive claim** — criminal defamation (a complaint before the magistrate, punishable with fine or imprisonment up to two years for convicted persons) and/or a civil suit for damages. Criminal defamation survived constitutional challenge; it is a slower instrument than people hope, which is why the interim application usually matters more.

Compensation follows documented harm: lost clients, lost offers, the cost of correction. Collect those numbers from the first week.

## If you are the one accused

A takedown demand is not a verdict. Assess honestly: is the statement true, or opinion on a true core? Truth and fair comment are defences; abuse is not. Remove or correct promptly where the claim has force, respond through a lawyer where it does not, and do not delete evidence — preservation obligations run both ways, and a destroyed post looks worse than a defended one.

## The trap to avoid

Do not sue the platform first and the poster never. The platform is a conduit with safe-harbour duties; the poster is the publisher. The sequence that works: poster's notice + platform grievance report in parallel, court against the poster (and the platform only where it ignored a compliant order or complaint).

## Your next move

1. Screenshot everything with URLs and timestamps; export before reporting.
2. Report to the platform's grievance officer and keep the ticket number.
3. Send the legal notice with a firm deadline for removal and retraction.
4. If reach is growing, ask a lawyer about an interim injunction this week, not this quarter.
5. Track the harm in numbers — the damages case is built on receipts, not feelings.
