## The short version

When a secured loan goes into default, the lender's powers come from the loan agreement and — for banks and notified financial institutions — from the SARFAESI Act, 2002. The Act's design surprises people: it hands the lender a fast, court-free enforcement track, but wraps every step in written notices with deadlines. Those deadlines are not decoration. They are the borrower's windows — to cure the default, to negotiate, to object, and to appeal.

The script, in order: **default → 13(2) demand notice (60 days) → 13(4) measures (possession symbolic or physical, takeover of management, sale) → Section 14 magistrate order for physical possession → sale notice (30 days minimum) → auction.** With an appeal to the Debt Recovery Tribunal sitting alongside from the 13(4) stage.

## The 13(2) notice: the 60 days that decide everything

The demand notice under Section 13(2) states the amount due and gives you **60 days** to discharge the liability. This is the single most misused window — most borrowers spend it in denial, and the file moves to enforcement untouched.

Use it instead:

1. **Check the arithmetic.** Compare the claimed amount with your statement — penal charges, compounded interest, and insurance add-ons are occasionally wrong. Any objection goes to the authorised officer **in writing** within the 60 days; the lender must consider objections to a 13(2) notice before proceeding.
2. **Make the proposal.** Restructuring, tenure extension, a one-time settlement — in writing, with your financials. Lenders have internal policies for hardship cases; a written proposal creates the record that you engaged, which matters at every later stage (and with every later officer).
3. **Do not ignore the notice** on the belief that "they can't take a house so easily". They can — through the script. The script just also gives you these two months.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Symbolic possession arrives before physical possession">
Under Section 13(4), lenders first take *symbolic* possession — a notice on the property, publicised. It is a legal milestone, not a lock-out. Physical possession of a house still needs a Section 14 order from the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate or District Magistrate. But symbolic possession is the moment to act, not to observe.
</Callout>

## The appeal: the DRT, within 45 days

Any person aggrieved by a Section 13(4) measure can appeal to the **Debts Recovery Tribunal** within **45 days**, on a deposit of 50% of the amount claimed (the tribunal can reduce it). Grounds include procedural violations — a defective notice, an unpaid objection, an amount wrongly computed — and the tribunal can stay the enforcement pending the appeal.

The DRT appeal is where the 60-day record pays: a borrower who objected in writing and proposed restructuring within the notice window walks in with a case; a borrower who spent the window silent walks in with sympathy and little else.

## The sale: notices, objections, and the auction

If enforcement reaches sale, further rights attach:

- **Sale notice** with at least 30 days' notice, published and served — check the date, the reserve price, and the mode.
- **Objections in writing**: undervaluation, procedural defects, anything misdescribed.
- **The redemption right**: until the sale is confirmed, the borrower can still pay the full dues and stop the sale — the amount is the principal, interest, and costs as claimed, which is why settlements before auction price differently than after.
- **Surplus belongs to you.** If the auction fetches more than the debt and costs, the excess is returnable — the Act is recovery, not forfeiture. Conversely, a shortfall remains recoverable.

## If the loan is unsecured

Personal and credit-card loans have no SARFAESI script: the lender's remedies are a civil suit, arbitration where the agreement provides it, and the [recovery-conduct rules](/guides/loan-recovery-harassment) that cap how collection feels. A "court notice" for a ₹60,000 card bill is usually an arbitration or civil notice, not a criminal one — default on a loan is not an offence. Read what actually arrived; panic pays extra.

## Your next move

1. Read the notice calmly and note every date it states.
2. Reconcile the claimed amount against your statements; object in writing within the window.
3. Make one concrete written proposal — restructuring or settlement — within the 60 days.
4. If a 13(4) measure hits, calendar the 45-day DRT deadline the same day; talk to a lawyer or legal aid about the pre-deposit.
5. Attend any auction process on paper: check the reserve price, object in writing, and know the redemption deadline.

<Callout tone="note" title="This is general information">
SARFAESI litigation is procedural and deadline-driven; tribunals and lenders differ in practice. For a live 13(2) notice or 13(4) possession, this guide is the orientation — a lawyer (or legal aid) should read your actual notices before deadlines run.
</Callout>
