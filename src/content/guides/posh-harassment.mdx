## The short version

The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 — the POSH Act — exists so that a complaint has a process that is faster, more private, and less intimidating than a police station or a civil suit. It requires every workplace with ten or more employees to constitute an **Internal Committee (IC)**, and it gives the IC a statutory method: written complaint, time-bound inquiry, interim reliefs, and recommendations to the employer.

Sexual harassment under the Act includes unwelcome physical contact and advances, a demand or request for sexual favours, sexually coloured remarks, showing pornography, and any other unwelcome physical, verbal, or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature. The operative word is **unwelcome** — not what the sender claims was intended, but what was experienced and communicated.

## Before you write the complaint

Write things down while they are fresh: dates, places, what was said or done, who saw or heard it. Save messages and emails — do not delete or "clean up" chats, even the parts that feel irrelevant. If a colleague witnessed anything, note their name now; memories of third parties fade fastest.

You do not need to write a legal document. You need a factual note: what happened, when, where, who, and what you did at the time. The IC's job is to turn that into an inquiry; your job is to give them the raw record.

## Filing with the Internal Committee

- **Where:** the IC of your workplace. If your workplace has fewer than ten employees, or the complaint is against the employer themselves, the **Local Committee** (Local Complaints Committee) at the district level hears it.
- **When:** within **three months** of the incident. The IC can extend this by another three months if it is satisfied the delay was genuine — late is not hopeless, but file as early as you can.
- **How:** in writing, to any IC member. If you cannot write it yourself, a member must help you record it. Ask for a dated acknowledgement of receipt.
- **Who can complain:** the aggrieved woman — an employee, intern, visitor, or contract worker at the workplace. If the aggrieved woman is unable to complain because of physical or mental incapacity, the Act lets others file on her behalf.

The Act also recognizes that **threats and retaliation** can accompany the harassment itself: victimisation after a complaint is expressly covered, and retaliation for filing is an offence.

## What the IC must do

1. Acknowledge and, within seven days, forward a copy to the respondent.
2. Complete the inquiry within **90 days**.
3. Give both sides a chance to be heard, to present documents and witnesses.
4. On completing the inquiry, send its findings and recommendations to the employer within **ten days**.
5. The employer must act on the recommendations within **60 days**.

The IC can recommend: compensation to you (which may be recovered from the respondent's salary), disciplinary action up to termination, deduction of the compensation from the respondent, and — during the inquiry — **interim relief**: transferring either party, granting you up to sixty days of paid leave, or restraining the respondent from supervising your work.

<Callout tone="tip" title="Ask for interim relief in the complaint itself">
If continuing to work under the respondent will punish you daily, say so in writing at the time of filing. The Act already allows a transfer of the respondent or paid leave for you — asking for it explicitly, with a sentence about why, is how it actually happens.
</Callout>

## Confidentiality is the default

The Act punishes breach of confidentiality — publishing or discussing the identity of the parties or the inquiry — with penalties on anyone involved, including IC members. The inquiry is not a public spectacle, and you can ask that proceedings be conducted to minimise contact between you and the respondent. Media exposure, office gossip, and WhatsApp leaks about an inquiry are not normal office behaviour; they are actionable.

## If the complaint is against your employer, or the workplace has no IC

The Local Committee exists for exactly this: workplaces with fewer than ten employees, complaints against the employer, or cases where the IC does not exist. The district officer designates a nodal officer in every block and taluka to receive complaints. The central and state governments also run the **SHe-Box** portal for online complaints — it routes to the right committee and gives you a tracking number.

If your employer has no IC despite having ten or more employees, note that failure: non-constitution of an IC is itself an offence, and it strengthens both your Local Committee complaint and any labour-department report.

## If you are a man, or the complainant is not a woman

The POSH Act's machinery is written for aggrieved women. A man (or any person) facing sexual harassment at work still has recourse: most employers' service rules and HR policies apply to everyone, and criminal law on stalking, voyeurism, and outraging modesty applies regardless of gender. Start with the company policy and, where needed, a police complaint. The absence of a POSH remedy is not the absence of remedies.

## The trap to avoid

The Act penalises **malicious** complaints — but it says explicitly that mere inability to prove the case is not maliciousness. Do not let fear of "what if I can't prove it" stop a truthful complaint. Conversely, do not embellish: every exaggeration is a gift to the defence, and truthful complainants do not need them.

## Your next move

1. Write the factual note now: dates, places, words, witnesses, screenshots.
2. Find the IC members (your employer must have notified them) or locate the Local Committee / SHe-Box.
3. File in writing, get a dated acknowledgement, and ask for interim relief.
4. Keep living your work life on the record — save everything, retaliate at nothing.
5. If the process stalls past its statutory clocks, escalate to the district officer and labour authorities.

POSH inquiries are individual proceedings with real consequences for everyone involved. This guide explains the statutory machinery; it is not a substitute for advice on your specific facts, especially where employment or reputation is at stake.
</Callout>
