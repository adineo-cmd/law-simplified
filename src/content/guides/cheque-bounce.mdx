## The short version

A bounced cheque is not just an inconvenience; under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act it is a criminal offence — with a procedure so clock-driven that the entire case can be won or lost in the paperwork days. The law's logic: a cheque is a promise to pay, and dishonour without prompt repair becomes criminal.

The four statutory conditions, in order:

1. A cheque drawn on an account for a **debt or liability**, issued to you.
2. Presented to the bank **within its validity period** (three months from the date on the cheque).
3. Dishonoured — the classic ground being "insufficient funds", but also signature mismatch, account closed, payment stopped.
4. The drawer fails to pay within **15 days** of your written demand notice.

Hit all four, and the dishonour becomes an offence punishable with imprisonment up to two years, or a fine up to twice the cheque amount, or both.

## Day zero: the cheque bounces

The bank returns the cheque with a **memo** stating the reason — "insufficient funds", "payment stopped by drawer", "account closed", "signature differs". Keep the original cheque and the memo together; they are exhibits one and two. Note the date you received the memo, because your first clock starts there.

A "payment stopped" memo does not defeat a Section 138 case by itself — courts look at whether the debt was real — but it signals a contested fight. Signature-mismatch returns are re-presentable: correct the error with the bank and present again, because each wrongful dishonour restarts the process.

## The demand notice: within 30 days

Within 30 days of receiving the memo, send a written **demand notice** to the drawer. The notice must:

- Identify the cheque (number, date, amount, bank).
- State the dishonour and the bank memo's reason.
- State the debt or liability the cheque was drawn for.
- Demand payment of the cheque amount **within 15 days of receipt of the notice** — the statutory phrase is "the said amount of money" paid to you.

Send it by registered post or speed post with acknowledgment due, and keep every postal receipt and tracking record. If the notice comes back undelivered, courts have generally treated proper dispatch to the correct address as sufficient service — which is exactly why the dispatch proof matters. <Callout tone="warning" title="The 30-day clock is strict">
Thirty days of *receiving* the bank memo. Not thirty days of the bounce, not thirty days after you got around to it. If the deadline is near, send the notice and fix the details later; a notice sent on day 29 with a small factual slip is recoverable, a notice sent on day 45 is not.
</Callout>

## The 15-day window, then the complaint

The drawer has 15 days **from receipt of the notice** to pay. This window is the law's off-ramp: pay and the offence never crystallises. Partial payment does not count — the demand is the cheque amount.

If the 15th day passes without full payment, the offence is complete, and you must file the complaint before the **magistrate** within **one month** of that expiry (the court can condone delay on sufficient cause, but do not bank on it). The complaint can be filed where your bank — the payee's branch — is located, among other connecting places, which means you often do not have to chase the drawer's city.

## What the case actually looks like

A Section 138 complaint is a summary trial: sworn complaint with documents, the court's process issued to the drawer, their plea, your evidence on affidavit, and cross-examination if contested. Many cases settle at the magistrate's door with the drawer paying principal, interest, and costs — the statute encourages compounding, and a settlement covering your real loss is a win.

Alongside the criminal case, you can sue for the money in a **civil suit** (the criminal case does not itself return the money; compensation there comes at conviction). A civil recovery suit has its own three-year limitation — see a lawyer about running both where the amount justifies it.

## The trap to avoid

Do not send the demand notice before you have your documents in order — the notice locks in your story. And do not accept a post-dated replacement cheque casually: taking a fresh cheque starts a new presentment cycle, and if you keep negotiating past the limitation for filing, you may talk yourself out of a case. Every extension of grace should be in writing, with the right reserved to proceed.

## Your next move

1. Secure the original cheque and the bank memo today; note the memo date.
2. Confirm the debt or liability is documented — invoice, agreement, ledger.
3. Send the demand notice within 30 days of the memo, by registered post, keeping every receipt.
4. Calendar day 15 (payment window) and the complaint deadline one month after.
5. File before the magistrate with the full paper trail; treat a fair settlement as victory.

<Callout tone="note" title="This is general information">
Section 138 litigation is procedural and deadline-driven; small factual differences change outcomes. For significant amounts, have a lawyer draft or review the demand notice — it is the document the whole case stands on.
</Callout>
