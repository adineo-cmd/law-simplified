## The short version

Most rent disputes are traced back to clauses the tenant never read, in a document the tenant signed in five minutes between the broker's calls. The agreement decides the deposit fight, the exit fight, and the repair fight. Reading it once, slowly, is the cheapest legal work you will ever do.

## Before you read it: verify the landlord

Ask for proof that the person signing owns the flat — the sale deed or the latest property tax receipt in their name. If the signer is a "caretaker" or an agent, ask for the owner's authorisation. Impersonated landlords are a real scam, and "I paid rent for two years to someone who did not own the flat" is a dispute you do not want.

Get the owner's ID (Aadhaar or PAN copy) and keep it with the agreement. You will need it for police verification, gas connection, and — if it ever comes to a fight — for the cause title of your notice.

## The five clauses that decide everything

**1. Notice and lock-in.** The notice clause says how early either side must speak before the tenancy ends. The lock-in says neither side can end the tenancy early at all — often 11 months. A lock-in binds both ways, but in practice tenants feel it most: leaving early can cost "rent in lieu of notice" or the deposit. If you might relocate for work, negotiate a lock-in with a buy-out (say, one month's rent) before signing, not after.

**2. The deposit.** The amount, and more importantly the refund wording: "refundable after adjusting dues and damages" without limits invites the [deduction fight](law-simplified/guides/security-deposit). Add, as an initial or annexure, the handover condition: photos or an inventory signed by both sides. That single annexure settles most end-of-tenancy arguments in advance.

**3. Maintenance and repairs.** Who pays for what: society maintenance is usually the owner's; interior repairs usually the tenant's; structural and appliance wear depends on the drafting. Check who pays the society's one-time charges, painting between tenants, and the RS/JMR style "move-in" fees some societies levy. An agreement silent on repairs defaults to the landlord's statutory duties — but silent drafting means arguments.

**4. Registration and stamp duty.** Many states require tenancy agreements above eleven months to be registered, and every state levies stamp duty on them. The common "11-month renewable" agreement exists partly to skip registration — legal where the law allows it, but weaker as evidence, and some states now require even short leases to be registered. Unregistered agreements that must have been registered can face problems when enforced. Budget the stamp paper and registration cost; ask who pays it, and put the answer in the agreement.

**5. The parties and the premises.** The exact names, the exact flat number, the exact address. Check who is named as landlord — an individual, a company, or "and sons". If the owner dies or sells mid-tenancy, your rights track the document, and a vague parties clause makes everything harder.

<Callout tone="warning" title="The 'guest restriction' clause">
Some agreements restrict overnight guests, require owner approval for visitors, or ban "non-vegetarian cooking" or specific communities. Clauses that violate constitutional fairness or personal liberty are challengeable, but arguing with a landlord you live under is expensive. If a clause feels invasive, negotiate it out before signing — that is the only cheap moment.
</Callout>

## The annexures worth demanding

- **Inventory and condition annexure:** furniture, appliances, meter readings, signed by both sides with dates.
- **Photograph set:** a shared album, timestamped, referenced in the agreement.
- **Owner's ID and ownership proof:** copies attached.
- **Broker terms:** the brokerage, who pays, and whether renewal brokerage applies. Brokers like to resell the same flat to you annually; a line saying renewal brokerage is not payable without fresh service is common sense.

## Renewals and increases

Rent escalation should be written: a percentage per renewal period, not "market rate at landlord's discretion". If the agreement says renewal "on mutually agreed terms", your negotiating power drops the month before expiry. Negotiate a formula — 5% per year is a common sane default.

## Your next move

1. Verify ownership before the token advance, not after.
2. Read the notice, lock-in, deposit, maintenance, and registration clauses out loud.
3. Negotiate the lock-in exit and the deposit refund wording before signing.
4. Attach the inventory and photo annexures on day one.
5. Pay the stamp duty and registration where required — it is evidence, not expense.

<Callout tone="note" title="This is general information">
Registration requirements, stamp duty rates, and rent law vary by state and change. Confirm your state's current requirement with the local sub-registrar or your District Legal Services Authority.
</Callout>
