## The short version

For years, Indian privacy fights ran on piecemeal rules — RBI circulars for banks, TRAI regulations for telecom, the IT Act's SPDI rules for everyone else. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP) gives individuals a statutory spine: notice, consent, correction, erasure, grievance, and a regulator. Where you see a company misusing your data, the machinery now exists to ask questions that must be answered.

The vocabulary matters for your letters: the company holding your data is the **data fiduciary**; you are the **data principal** (or the parent/guardian acting for a child).

## What the Act gives you

- **Notice:** before collecting personal data, the fiduciary must tell you what it collects and for what purpose — in plain language, with a way to contact them.
- **Consent, and its withdrawal:** consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, for a clearly stated purpose. You can withdraw it, with the caveat that withdrawal may end the service that depended on it.
- **Access and correction:** you can ask for a summary of your data and the processing activities, and require correction of inaccurate or incomplete data.
- **Erasure:** you can ask for erasure of your data once the purpose is served and no legal retention requirement applies.
- **Grievance redressal:** every fiduciary must publish a grievance redressal mechanism, and respond before you can escalate.
- **The Data Protection Board:** if the fiduciary does not respond or you are unsatisfied, complaints go to the Data Protection Board of India — the enforcement body the Act creates.

## The letter that does the work

Rights under the Act are exercised in writing. A good data request is boring and specific:

- Who you are: name, registered email/phone, account or customer ID.
- What you ask: (a) a summary of the personal data held and the purposes, (b) correction of [specific error], (c) erasure of [specific data] as its purpose is served.
- The clock: a response to the grievance within a stated, reasonable period.
- The escalation: "failing which I will take the grievance to the Data Protection Board".

Use the [data correction and erasure request](law-simplified/toolkit#dpdp-request) template. One request, one purpose; a scattershot "delete everything" demand invites a refusal based on legal retention duties.

## What companies can still lawfully keep

Erasure is not amnesia. Companies must retain records where other laws say so: tax and accounting records, KYC norms for financial services, transaction logs under telecom rules, medical records under clinical establishments norms. That is why the smart erasure request targets the data whose purpose is genuinely served — old marketing profiles, closed-account analytics — while acknowledging statutory retention. A request that shows you know the difference is harder to dismiss.

## Banking, telecom and the older shields

The DPDP Act sits on top of sectoral regimes that came first and remain in force:

- **Banks and finance:** RBI's customer-protection and digital-lending norms limit data use and mandate fair practices; the [banking ombudsman](law-simplified/guides/insurance-claim) machinery handles disputes.
- **Telecom:** TRAI's rules govern subscriber data and unsolicited communication — DND registrations and spam complaints run through them.
- **Everyone else:** the IT Act's reasonable-security rules for sensitive data still apply where relevant.

When you complain, cite the strongest applicable rule, not just the newest Act. A fintech app mishandling data answers to RBI and DPDP both.

## If your data leaked

A breach is not just an embarrassment; under the Act a fiduciary must notify affected data principals and the Board. Practical steps, in order: change credentials (especially if passwords are reused), enable two-factor authentication, screenshot the breach notification you received, ask in writing what data was exposed, and watch your financial statements. For financial fraud that follows a leak, the [cyber-fraud first hour](law-simplified/guides/cyber-fraud) applies — speed there matters more than any privacy letter.

## The trap to avoid

Do not sign away rights you did not read. Consent notices are long, but the parts that matter are the purposes and the third-party sharing list. A photograph uploaded today is a profile picture of record for years — the cheapest privacy control remains collecting less: an email alias for signups, a secondary number, permissions revoked on apps that do not need them.

## Your next move

1. List where your data lives and which accounts you no longer use.
2. Send written access/correction/erasure requests to the worst offenders.
3. Withdraw consent for marketing — the unsubscribe and DND routes exist for this.
4. Escalate unanswered grievances: Data Protection Board for general data; RBI or TRAI for their sectors.
5. Change credentials and enable 2FA before writing any letter about a breach.
