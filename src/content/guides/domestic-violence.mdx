## The short version

Domestic violence law in India is broader than most people assume — in two directions. The conduct covered is broad: physical abuse, sexual abuse, verbal and emotional abuse (insults, ridicule, humiliation, name-calling especially about not bearing a child), and **economic abuse** — denying money you are entitled to, disposing of your stridhan, withholding maintenance. And the relationships covered are broad: any "domestic relationship" — marriage, live-in relationships in the nature of marriage, family members in a shared household.

The Act's machinery is civil. That is its strength: a magistrate can grant **protection orders, residence orders, monetary relief, custody arrangements, and compensation** quickly, without a criminal trial. A parallel criminal complaint (cruelty under Section 85/86 BNS, formerly 498A IPC) is possible when the conduct is criminal, but the DV Act works on its own.

## What the four main orders actually do

- **Protection order:** forbids the respondent from committing abuse, entering your workplace or home, contacting you, or influencing you. Breach is a criminal offence — the Act makes violation punishable, which is where the civil shield grows teeth.
- **Residence order:** you cannot be evicted from a **shared household** — and the magistrate can restrain the respondent from dispossessing you, or direct them to secure alternative accommodation, or pay the rent. The house does not have to be in your name; "shared household" is the test, and the Supreme Court's *Satish Chander Ahuja* decision settled that it includes a household belonging to the husband's family where the couple lived.
- **Monetary relief:** for maintenance, medical expenses, loss of earnings, and the return of stridhan.
- **Interim/ex parte orders:** the magistrate can grant relief the same day, before the respondent is even heard, where immediate danger exists.

## How the process actually starts

You do not need a lawyer to begin:

1. **Inform a Protection Officer.** Every district has them (usually in the Women and Child or Social Welfare department). They must record the Domestic Incident Report, forward your application to the magistrate, and arrange shelter, medical care, and legal aid.
2. **File directly before the magistrate** (Judicial Magistrate First Class / Metropolitan Magistrate) of the area where you live, where the respondent lives, or where the abuse occurred.
3. **Through a service provider** — registered organisations that can record the incident and file for you.
4. **By phone first:** the 181 women's helpline and 112 dispatch help and connect you to the Protection Officer. A police station can also record your complaint, but the DV Act's forum is the magistrate, not the station house.

The application states the facts, the relationship, and the orders sought. Medical reports, photographs, messages, and witnesses support it — but their absence does not block a filing; the Domestic Incident Report and your testimony start the case.

<Callout tone="warning" title="If tonight is the danger">
If you are in immediate danger, leave with identity documents, some cash, and any medication if you can. 181 and 112 exist for this hour. Shelter homes (Swadhar Greh, One Stop Centres) take women and accompanying children without a court case. Safety first, paperwork after.
</Callout>

## Evidence you should start collecting

- Dates and a short factual note for each incident — what happened, what was said, who was present.
- Injuries: medical examination and the MLC (medico-legal case) record, photographed.
- Messages, emails, call logs — preserved, not just screenshotted; export the originals.
- Financial evidence: whose name the accounts are in, what you contributed, stridhan bills and photographs.
- Witnesses: neighbours, domestic help, family — names and numbers now, while memories are fresh.

## What happens at the magistrate's

The magistrate fixes an early date (the Act directs the first hearing within three days of receiving the application, with the DIF in hand). Interim orders are common at this stage. The respondent is then served and heard; a Protection Officer's report and both sides' evidence follow. The tone of the Act is protective: ex parte interim orders, in-camera proceedings, and penalties for breaching orders.

## The trap to avoid

Do not sign anything at the police station or with family "panchayat" intermediaries that waives your right to approach a magistrate — such settlements have no legal value and are used to run out the clock. And do not equate "no visible injury" with "no case": economic and verbal abuse are explicitly covered, and the Domestic Incident Report is your record either way.

## Your next move

1. If in danger now: 181 or 112, then a One Stop Centre or shelter.
2. Record the incident history in date order while it is fresh.
3. Locate your district's Protection Officer (the WCD department or DC's office lists them).
4. File the DV application — with legal aid if you cannot afford a lawyer — and ask for interim protection and residence orders.
5. Where the conduct is criminal, discuss a parallel BNS cruelty complaint with your lawyer; the two run on separate tracks.
