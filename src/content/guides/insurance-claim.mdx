## The short version

An insurance company is a business that profits by collecting premiums and pays claims under a contract. When it rejects a claim, it must give you the reasons in writing. That letter — the repudiation letter — tells you exactly what you have to argue against. Read it before you feel defeated by it.

Most rejections fall into three buckets:

- **Documentation:** forms missing, bills incomplete, discharge summary not attached, claim filed outside the notice window.
- **Waiting periods and exclusions:** pre-existing disease waits, specific illness waits, or an exclusion clause the insurer says applies.
- **Non-disclosure:** the insurer says you did not disclose a material fact — usually a pre-existing condition — when the policy began.

Each bucket has a different reply, and the first one is often just paperwork you can fix.

## Step one: get the reasons in writing

If the rejection came as an SMS or a call, ask for the repudiation letter with the exact policy conditions relied upon. An insurer that cannot point to a clause is arguing, not deciding. Ask for the full claim file notes too — you are entitled to the basis of the decision, and the request itself signals that you will escalate properly.

## Step two: answer the letter, clause by clause

Match each stated reason with a document or an argument:

- **"Records incomplete."** Send the exact missing item with a covering email naming the claim number. Ask for a fresh assessment within 15 days.
- **"Waiting period applies."** Check the policy schedule and the date of the first symptom in your medical records. Waiting periods are counted from policy inception — if you bought the policy years ago, the wait may already have expired.
- **"Pre-existing condition not disclosed."** This is the most contested ground. The test is materiality: did the proposal form actually ask about that condition, and did you know of it? A condition you genuinely did not know or was not asked about is not concealment. Old policy wording matters — get the proposal form copy.

<Callout tone="warning" title="Do not sign anything that closes the claim">
Insurers sometimes offer a partial payment "in full and final settlement". If you sign, you generally cannot claim the balance later. Accept a part-payment only in writing marked "without prejudice to my claim for the balance", or refuse and escalate.
</Callout>

## Step three: escalate on the record

The insurance industry has a defined complaint ladder:

1. **The insurer's grievance cell.** Every insurer must have one; use the email on the policy document and keep the ticket number.
2. **IRDAI's Bima Bharosa portal.** Register the complaint against the insurer with the claim number. The regulator's registration often shakes loose stalled files, though IRDAI itself does not adjudicate your claim.
3. **The Insurance Ombudsman.** For claims disputes, this is the fast track. It is free, documents-based, and designed for people without lawyers. The award binds the insurer (up to the ombudsman's monetary limit) if you accept it.
4. **Consumer Commission.** For larger amounts or complex disputes, a deficiency-in-service complaint under the Consumer Protection Act works — insurance disputes are standard consumer filings.

## Keep the medical trail

For health claims, your hospital file is the case: the admission note, the first-treatment date, the discharge summary, and every bill. Collect the complete set from the hospital's records department with their stamp. For life claims, the nominee's documents — death certificate, KYC, bank details — should be one clean PDF set.

## The trap to avoid

Do not wait for the "claim status" to change on its own. Claims die quietly in queues, and limitation clocks run. Every escalation gets a number; collect them. An ombudsman filing with three ticket numbers and dated letters reads very differently from an angry email with none.

## Your next move

1. Get the repudiation letter with the specific clauses relied upon.
2. Build the document set: policy, proposal form, claim form, medical or incident records, correspondence.
3. Reply in writing, clause by clause, with a 15-day resolution ask.
4. Escalate through the grievance cell, then Bima Bharosa, then the Ombudsman.
5. Keep every ticket number in one file — that file is your case.

Insurance contracts are individual documents and ombudsman rules evolve. This guide explains the machinery, not the outcome of your specific policy — for large claims, a consumer lawyer's opinion on the repudiation letter is money well spent.
</Callout>
