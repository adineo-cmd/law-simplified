## The short version

You paid for something. It did not work, did not arrive, or was not what was promised. The law does not treat that as bad luck you have to absorb. The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 gives you a process — but the process rewards people who keep records and ask for a specific remedy.

There are three common lanes:

- **A defective product.** It has a fault, is unsafe, or does not meet the quality or performance it claims.
- **A deficiency in service.** A service was not performed, was performed badly, or was performed late compared with what was promised.
- **An unfair trade practice.** The seller misled you, hid a charge, made a false claim, or refused to honour a warranty.

Your complaint should say which lane you are in. “It is a scam” is a feeling. “The seller advertised a two-year warranty and is refusing to repair a manufacturing defect within that period” is a claim.

## Start with a written complaint

Before you escalate, create a paper trail. A phone call is hard to prove; an email with an attachment is not.

Send the seller a short letter or email that includes:

1. What you bought, when, and for how much.
2. The order number and invoice.
3. What went wrong, with dates.
4. What you have already tried.
5. The remedy you want, and a deadline.

Our [consumer complaint template](/toolkit#consumer-complaint) is ready to copy. Keep the tone factual. You do not need to threaten a lawsuit in the first email; you need a record that you asked and they had a chance to fix it.

<Callout tone="tip" title="Ask for one remedy at a time">
“Repair, replace, or refund” is clear. “Please do something” is not. If you are open to alternatives, say so, but make the order of preference explicit.
</Callout>

## If the seller stalls

The National Consumer Helpline is a sensible first escalation. It is a mediation and grievance platform, not a court. You can register a complaint online and track it. Many sellers respond when a complaint gets a reference number.

If that does not work, the Consumer Commission is the formal route. Complaints can be filed online through e-Daakhil. The commission will look at whether the product or service was defective, whether the loss is proved, and what relief is fair.

A few practical points:

- **Limitation.** A consumer complaint generally has to be filed within two years of the cause of action. The date can be disputed, so do not wait until the last month.
- **Jurisdiction.** The commission depends on the value of the claim and where the seller or you are located. The e-Daakhil filing flow will ask for the relevant details; when in doubt, ask the commission’s registry or a legal aid clinic.
- **Evidence.** The invoice, warranty, payment proof, photographs, service reports, and messages matter more than a long story.
- **Compensation.** You can ask for the loss you actually suffered, and in some cases compensation for mental agony and litigation costs. It is not automatic, and it is not a lottery.

## What to keep

Make a folder — digital or physical — with:

- invoice and order confirmation;
- warranty or guarantee card;
- payment proof: bank statement, UPI screenshot, card slip;
- photographs or a video of the defect;
- every email, chat, and call log;
- the seller’s written response, or proof that they did not respond;
- a copy of your complaint and its acknowledgement.

If the product is unsafe, stop using it and preserve the condition. Do not repair it yourself before the seller has a chance to inspect it unless it is an emergency.

## The trap to avoid

A replacement or a partial refund may be a good outcome. It may also be offered on the condition that you give up the rest of your claim. Before you accept, ask:

- Is this full and final?
- Does it cover the whole loss, including delivery or installation charges?
- What happens to the warranty?
- Will you confirm the settlement in writing?

You can accept a fair settlement. Just do not sign away a larger claim without understanding it.

## Your next move

1. Write down the facts in date order.
2. Send the written complaint with a specific remedy and a 15-day deadline.
3. Register a National Consumer Helpline complaint if the seller does not respond.
4. File before the Consumer Commission if the value and the evidence justify it.
5. If you cannot afford a lawyer, ask your District Legal Services Authority about free legal aid.
