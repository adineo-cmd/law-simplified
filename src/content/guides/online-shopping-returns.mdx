## The short version

When an online order goes wrong, you have four lanes, and they are not sequential: the platform's return flow, the grievance officer required by the e-commerce rules, your bank's chargeback process, and the consumer commission. Platforms count on you using only the first one and giving up.

The rules that matter: the Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 require every e-commerce entity to publish a grievance officer's name and details, acknowledge complaints within **48 hours**, and dispose of them within **one month**. Marketplace platforms must also display the seller's details — the entity that actually sold you the item.

## Before you return anything

Screenshot the listing page — price, delivery promise, return window, and warranty claims — before you ship the item back. Product pages change, and your screenshots are the record of what you were promised when you paid.

While returning: photograph the item and the packing, keep the courier receipt, and write down the pickup or drop date. If the item was damaged on arrival, photograph it **before** the pickup agent arrives, not after.

## Lane one: the platform's own flow

Raise the return or refund inside the app, choose the correct reason code, and paste a two-line factual summary into the comments box: what was ordered, what arrived, when. The reason code drives their policy engine, so pick the one that matches reality rather than the one that seems fastest.

If the platform offers "wallet credit" instead of a refund to source, you can refuse it. A refund to the original payment method is the normal expectation for a cancelled or returned order, and wallet credit is a convenience for the platform, not a right they can impose.

## Lane two: the grievance officer

If the flow stalls, email the grievance officer — their details must be on the platform's site — with the order number, the dates, and a specific ask: "refund of ₹X to source by [date]". The 48-hour and one-month clocks start when they acknowledge. When you later file on a helpline or before a commission, this email is what shows the platform failed its own legal clock.

## Lane three: the chargeback

For card, netbanking, or UPI payments, your bank can dispute the transaction — "chargeback" for cards, and for UPI there is a dispute mechanism in the app plus the bank's nodal officer. Grounds include non-delivery, item materially different from the description, and a merchant who took payment and went silent.

Raise it early: card networks have time limits that are often shorter than people assume — typically 120 days from the transaction or the expected delivery date. A chargeback does not require the seller's cooperation, which is exactly why it is worth running in parallel rather than after the platform's process dies.

<Callout tone="tip" title="A prepaid order that never arrived">
For non-delivery of a prepaid order, the chargeback is usually clean: payment proof, order confirmation, delivery promise, and the platform's own tracking showing no delivery. This is the highest-success dispute most banks see.
</Callout>

## Lane four: escalation

If the platform and the bank both stall, register on the National Consumer Helpline, and if needed file before the District Commission — see the [step-by-step filing guide](law-simplified/guides/file-consumer-complaint). Claims for online purchases are among the most common consumer filings, and the documents carry the case.

## The trap to avoid

Do not let the return window close while you argue. Raise the return on time even if the refund conversation is unresolved — a late return converts a straightforward claim into an avoidable argument. And never ship back the item without recording its condition; "item not as returned" is the seller's easiest defence.

## Your next move

1. Screenshot the listing, order confirmation, and return policy today.
2. Raise the return inside the platform and note the reason code.
3. Email the grievance officer if the first response misses the 48-hour clock.
4. Open the bank dispute for prepaid orders in parallel, not after.
5. Escalate to the helpline and the commission only after the trail is documented.

<Callout tone="note" title="This is general information">
Platform policies and card-network chargeback rules change frequently. This guide is the durable part — the clocks, the lanes, and the paperwork — not a substitute for reading the policy on your own order.
</Callout>
