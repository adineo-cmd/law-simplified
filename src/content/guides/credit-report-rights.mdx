## The short version

A credit score is downstream data: lenders report your loan conduct to credit bureaus monthly, and the bureaus compute the score from those reports. When the report is wrong — a loan you closed still showing "open", an EMI marked late that the bank's own app shows paid, an account you never opened — the bureau did not invent the error; a lender reported it. That is why the dispute process has two gears, and why using only the first one disappoints people.

## What is actually in the report

Read the report like a lender would, section by section:

- **Personal and contact details** — a wrong phone number or address can attach someone else's loan to your file, or yours to theirs.
- **Accounts** — for each: open date, type, current balance, and the **Days Past Due (DPD)** history in columns. This grid is the score's main fuel.
- **Enquiries** — every time a lender pulls your report. A dozen enquiries in two months reads as credit-hungry and drops the score even with no new loan.
- **Status tags** — "standard", "settled", "written-off", "post (WO) settled". These are the entries that outlive the loan: "settled" tells every future lender you paid less than owed, and it stays until the lender changes it.

## The dispute machinery

1. **Get all the reports, not one.** Each bureau is entitled to one free full report per year. The error often appears in only one bureau's file — because only that bureau received the bad report.
2. **File the dispute online** with the bureau (CIBIL's dispute form, Experian's, CRIF's — all online). Flag the exact account and the exact field, and attach proof: the closure letter, the NOC, bank statements showing the EMI cleared, the settlement receipt.
3. **The bureau routes it to the lender.** Under the Credit Information Companies Rules, the bureau must forward the dispute to the reporting lender; the lender responds (or corrects its feed), and the bureau updates. The bureau's own clock is days; the lender's diligence is the variable.
4. **Track the dispute ID** and re-pull the report after 30 days.

## When the lender is the problem

If the bureau's answer is "lender did not respond" or "lender confirms the entry", the dispute moves to the lender — and the escalation is the one that works everywhere in finance: the bank's grievance cell with the branch's written replies attached, then the **RBI Ombudsman** at cms.rbi.org.in, which handles credit-information complaints. Ombudsman directions forcing a lender to correct a bureau feed are well established.

Keep the physical proof the whole process runs on: loan closure letters and NOCs (demand them at closure — never assume), settlement receipts, and the final statement showing zero.

## Identity theft is a different case

An account in your name that you never opened is not a data error — it is fraud:

1. File a police complaint (cybercrime.gov.in for online fraud) naming the lender and the account.
2. Dispute with the bureau attaching the complaint acknowledgement.
3. Write to the lender demanding the account's KYC documents — the paper trail of how "you" opened it usually ends the argument.
4. Check whether your data leaked from somewhere you used — the [DPDP rights](/guides/data-privacy) apply to the entity that mishandled it.

## Habits that keep the score boring

Pay before the due date, not on it — DPD columns record a cycle, and edge-of-due-date payments land late more often than people expect. Keep old credit cards open (length of history helps). Space loan applications. And pull your free reports annually — the cheapest credit repair is catching the wrong entry the year it appears, not the month you apply for a home loan.

## Your next move

1. Pull your free report from each of the four bureaus this month.
2. List every wrong field, per bureau, with the proof for each.
3. File online disputes per bureau and keep the dispute IDs.
4. Where a lender stonewalls, run the grievance-cell → RBI Ombudsman ladder.
5. Set an annual reminder to re-pull the reports — errors are recurring, not rare.
